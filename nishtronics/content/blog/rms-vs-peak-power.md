Pick up almost any speaker box and you will see two power numbers, something like "350W peak / 90W RMS". The big one is easy to love. The small one is the one that matters.

## What the two numbers mean

**RMS** is the power a speaker can handle continuously, for hours, without overheating or damage. It is the realistic number.

**Peak** is the most power it can survive in a very short burst, a fraction of a second. Nothing about normal listening looks like that, so peak tells you very little about how the speaker will behave in your car.

That is why the power meter on each speaker, subwoofer and amplifier in our shop uses the RMS figure and not the peak one.

## Matching an amplifier to a speaker

The goal is clean power that roughly matches what the speaker can take. A common rule of thumb is to choose an amplifier that delivers between about 75% and 150% of the speaker's RMS rating, measured at the speaker's impedance.

So a pair of speakers rated at 90 W RMS each sits comfortably with an amplifier giving 70 to 130 W per channel.

Two things people get wrong:

- **Too little power is not the safe option.** A small amplifier pushed to loud volumes starts to clip, which sends distorted signal to the speaker. Clipping damages speakers faster than a larger, clean amplifier does.
- **Impedance changes the answer.** Amplifier ratings are different at 4 ohms and 2 ohms, and often higher into the lower load. Always read the rating at the impedance you will actually wire.

## Your head unit is not a 50-watt amplifier

Many head units advertise "50W x 4". That is a peak figure. The real continuous output is usually much lower, often somewhere around 15 to 20 watts RMS per channel. It is plenty for factory speakers at moderate volume, and it is the reason a separate amplifier makes such a difference.

## Sensitivity matters too

Two speakers with the same RMS rating can play at very different volumes. Sensitivity, measured in decibels, tells you how loud a speaker gets from one watt. A higher number means more volume per watt, which helps if you are working with a modest amplifier.

## A quick checklist

1. Ignore the peak number when you compare products.
2. Note each speaker's RMS rating and impedance.
3. Choose an amplifier whose RMS output at that impedance is close to it.
4. Set the gain properly when it is installed, so the amplifier does not clip.
