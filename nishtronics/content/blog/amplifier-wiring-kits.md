A wiring kit looks like the boring part of an audio system. It is actually the part that can start a fire if it is chosen or fitted badly.

## What AWG means

AWG is a wire gauge, and the number runs backwards. **A smaller number means a thicker cable.** A 4 AWG cable is thicker than an 8 AWG cable and carries more current with less voltage loss.

Thin cable for a powerful amplifier gets hot and wastes power. The amplifier is starved, sounds weaker and can cut out at higher volume.

## Which size do you need?

It depends on the amplifier's total power and the length of the run. As a rough guide for a typical efficient amplifier:

| Total amplifier power (RMS) | Typical power cable |
|---|---|
| Up to about 500 W | 8 AWG |
| About 500 to 1,000 W | 4 AWG |
| Above that | Thicker cable, or a different plan |

These are approximate. The amplifier's manual usually has its own chart, and it takes priority. If you are between sizes, go thicker.

## Copper and copper-clad aluminium

Some cheaper kits use copper-clad aluminium (CCA), where an aluminium core is coated in copper. It conducts less than the same size of pure copper, so a CCA cable should be a size up to do the same job. Oxygen-free copper (OFC) is the better choice and runs cooler.

## The fuse

Fit the main fuse close to the battery, within about 30 cm of the terminal. Its job is to protect the cable. If the cable shorts to the body somewhere along its route, the fuse is what stops it from heating up. The fuse rating should not exceed what the cable can carry.

## The ground

A short ground cable to clean, bare metal is as important as the power cable. Keep it as short as practical, sand off paint at the contact point and tighten it properly. A bad ground causes noise, heat and strange behaviour.

## Routing

- Pass cables through the firewall using a rubber grommet, never bare against the metal edge.
- Keep the power cable away from the signal (RCA) cables, or the system will pick up alternator whine.
- Secure the cable so it cannot move, rub or be pinched.

## Bigger systems

Above a certain power the factory wiring in the engine bay becomes the weak point. Upgrading the main battery, alternator and ground cables, often called the "big three", helps a large system get the current it needs.
